# Sweeping rho fills the missing L2 measurement

No published experiment has measured the per-bit upset rate of a GPU's L2 cache and its GDDR on the same device. Nothing at all exists for Ada/AD102, TSMC 4N SRAM or GDDR6X. The ratio rho = lambda_SRAM_per_bit / lambda_DRAM_per_bit therefore has to be built from mismatched sources, and it should be swept rather than fixed. The evidence depends strongly on the particle spectrum. For terrestrial neutrons, FinFET cache SRAM measures about **2–20 FIT/Mb** and modern DRAM cells about **0.001–0.0125 FIT/Mb**, which puts **rho near 10^3 (plausible range ~10^2 to ~10^4)**. The only same-GPU field comparison (Titan K20X, 28 nm, ECC on) gives **rho ≈ 40–260** once ten defective cards are removed. Heavy-ion data, relevant to space, pull the ratio down to **≈0.1–2**. The defensible recommendation is a log sweep of **rho ∈ {1, 10, 100, 1000}**: 1000 is the terrestrial-neutron central value, 100 is the field-consistent value, and 1 is the heavy-ion and memory-agnostic (REMU-style) floor. There is one structural catch that matters as much as the value of rho. Setting BER_cache = rho × BER_DRAM is only correct if L2 and DRAM receive the **same fluence within a trial**. The campaign's DRAM BER is an accumulated, persistent quantity, while cache flips live only as long as their line stays resident. A physically faithful model therefore multiplies by a time-window ratio. Under space-like accumulation rates, that factor makes cache upsets per inference negligible. The rho sweep should be presented as an equal-fluence stress bound, sampled with Poisson rather than `round()`, and summarized by the crossover rho* at which cache faults start to matter.

## No study has measured L2 and GDDR on the same GPU

The gap is structural rather than accidental. The two groups that produce most GPU beam data, UFRGS/Rech and NVIDIA, deliberately keep DRAM out of the experiment. Fermi and Kepler boards were irradiated with a 2-inch spot "without directly affecting nearby board power control circuitry and DDR memories". SRAM cross sections were published only **normalized to the K20 L2**, with absolute values withheld ([Rech et al. SELSE'14](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~skeckler/pubs/SELSE_2014_Reliability.pdf)). Later Kepler and Volta campaigns state that "this evaluation considers errors occurring in the GPU core, not in the main memory". They either kept the GDDR outside the beam or triplicated global memory, and normalized FIT by an undisclosed constant "not to reveal business-sensitive data" ([Fernandes dos Santos thesis, UFRGS 2022](https://lume.ufrgs.br/bitstream/handle/10183/234971/001136966.pdf); [Fernandes dos Santos & Rech 2024](https://d-nb.info/1334458731/34)). NVIDIA's only GPU-DRAM beam paper irradiated HBM2 on a V100 at ChipIR with DRAM ECC off. Its plotted per-Gb rate is normalized. The **12.51 FIT/Gb** used in its system analysis is an **assumption** "inspired by the GDDR5 memory failure rates in the Titan supercomputer", not a measurement, even though search engines often misreport it as one ([Sullivan et al. MICRO'21](https://www.mbsullivan.info/attachments/papers/sullivan2021characterizing.pdf)).

The fault-injection methodology literature does not fill the gap either. Li et al. (SC17) apply a single raw rate, **20.49 FIT/Mb**, to latches and on-chip SRAM buffers. That rate is extrapolated from a 28 nm SRAM beam result quoted as "157.62 FIT/MB", and since 157.62/8 ≈ 19.7, the "16 nm projection" may simply be a unit conversion of the 28 nm value. The paper explicitly excludes main memory ([Li et al. SC17](https://research.nvidia.com/sites/default/files/pubs/2017-11_Understanding-Error-Propagation/SC17_DNN_Resilience.pdf)). gpuFI-4 applies one per-bit value (**1.8×10⁻⁶ FIT/bit at 12 nm, 1.2×10⁻⁵ at 28 nm**) identically to the register file, shared memory, L1 and L2, and it does not model DRAM ([Sartzetakis et al. ISPASS'22](https://www.ceid.upatras.gr/webpages/faculty/gpapad/assets/papers/ispass2022_sartzetakis.pdf)). NVIDIA's two-level SDC model ran its beam tests with ECC on for RF, caches and DRAM, so it yields no raw rate for either ([Hari et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.01445)). REMU, the closest memory-aware DNN injector, applies a memory-agnostic **10⁻⁷–10⁻⁶ /bit/day** to LPDDR4. That figure comes from CARMEN-2 flight memories and a 28 nm SRAM-FPGA neutron test, so it implicitly treats rho as 1 ([REMU, DAC 2025](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/iel8/11132383/11132091/11132935.pdf); [RedNet arXiv 2407.11853](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.11853)). No examined paper states an "SRAM = k × DRAM per bit" constant.

## Terrestrial neutrons put L2 near a thousandfold above DRAM

The node-scaling evidence is the strongest basis for a terrestrial rho. On the SRAM side, Xilinx's reliability report shows dense block RAM falling from **66 FIT/Mb at 28 nm planar to 20 FIT/Mb at 16 nm FinFET** in real-time NYC-corrected tests. Its neutron cross section dropped from 5.57×10⁻¹⁵ to 9.82×10⁻¹⁶ cm²/bit ([Xilinx UG116](https://0x04.net/~mwk/xidocs/ug/drr.pdf)). A true logic-process cache, the ARM caches on the 16 nm UltraScale+ MPSoC, measured **1.5×10⁻¹⁵ cm²/bit**, or about 20 FIT/Mb at the JESD89A reference flux ([Agiakatsikas et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.08098)). TSMC reports that SER is "significantly reduced in 7nm SRAM" ([Fang & Oates IRPS'18](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/8353584/)). The trend then reverses: scaling to 5 nm FinFET "results in an increase in the per-bit SER relative to the 7-nm FinFET process", and fast process corners run up to 2× higher ([Narasimham et al. IRPS'23](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/10118025/)). For low-LET particles the 5 nm increase is about 2× over 7 nm ([Pieper et al. TNS'23](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/2004056)). No absolute N7, N5 or N4 number is public, so placing an AD102 L2 at **roughly 2–20 FIT/Mb** is an inference: the same order as 16 nm FinFET, bracketed by the 7 nm dip and the 5 nm rebound.

The DRAM side fell much further. Baumann documents that "the DRAM SER of a single bit is shrinking about 4× to 5× per generation" and had dropped by more than 1,000× over seven generations, while SRAM bit SER "has reached saturation" ([Baumann, IEEE D&T 2005](http://www.cs.columbia.edu/~cs4823/handouts/baumann-soft-errors-DT-05.pdf)). Slayman's review puts current DRAM cells at **10⁻⁹–10⁻⁸ FIT/bit (0.001–0.01 FIT/Mb)**; that wording comes from search summaries and was not verified against the PDF ([Slayman, RAMS 2011](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/224231257_Soft_error_trends_and_mitigation_techniques_in_memory_devices)). NVIDIA describes its measured HBM2 rate as "within expectations given the historical trends" ([Sullivan et al. MICRO'21](https://www.mbsullivan.info/attachments/papers/sullivan2021characterizing.pdf)). Dividing 2–20 FIT/Mb by 0.001–0.0125 FIT/Mb gives **rho ≈ 160–20,000**. The geometric centre is about 1,800, and the commonly paired values (20 FIT/Mb against 12.51 FIT/Gb) give about 1,600. History supports the direction: a 2004 compilation placed SRAM and DRAM in the same 1,000–2,000 FIT/Mbit band and already noted that "SRAM has become more susceptible to soft errors than DRAM" ([Tezzaron 2004](https://tezzaron.com/media/soft_errors_1_1_secure.pdf)). Rho was about 1 in that era and has grown by roughly three decades since. No GDDR6 or GDDR6X beam data exist, and Micron publishes none. Treating GDDR6X cells as DDR4/HBM2-class is an inference, and the PAM4 interface and periphery could add non-cell error modes.

The only same-device check comes from the field and lands lower. On Titan, 98% of corrected SBEs fell in the 1.5 MB L2, but "close to 98% of all SBEs occurred on only ten cards". After those cards are excluded, "the device memory is the structure where most of the SBEs occur (96% of all SBEs)" ([Tiwari et al. HPCA'15](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/1185857)). Normalizing by capacity (12 Mibit L2 against 48 Gibit GDDR5, a ratio of 4,096) gives **rho ≤ 170 (text) or ≤ 260 (figure)** for L2 alone. Spreading the residual over all SECDED SRAM gives **44–67**. Including the outlier cards gives about 2×10⁵, which is a weak-cell or defect signature, not a soft-error ratio. Titan DBEs give a similar register-file-to-GDDR5 ratio of about 285 (86% of DBEs in GPU memory, the rest in the RF) ([Ostrouchov et al. SC20, summarizing Tiwari SC15](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/1771896)). The field number is biased in both directions. Hard and intermittent DRAM faults inflate the denominator. ECC only sees bits that are read, and L2 is read continuously while most GDDR sits idle, which inflates the numerator. These field rhos are also at 28 nm planar, where SRAM was about 3× worse per bit than at 16 nm FinFET. So the field value of ~10² and the component value of ~10³ are not in conflict. Together they bound terrestrial rho to **10²–10⁴, with 10³ the best single estimate**.

Within the GPU, the L2 is a reasonable stand-in for all on-chip SRAM. On K20, per-bit RF/L2 ratios are **0.81–1.13 (pattern-averaged 0.90)**. L2 bits holding 0 are about 40% more sensitive than bits holding 1. Fermi cells were 2.1–2.4× more sensitive than Kepler cells ([Rech et al. SELSE'14](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~skeckler/pubs/SELSE_2014_Reliability.pdf)). Kepler's planar RF has "approximately an order of magnitude higher error rate than Volta RF" on FinFET ([Fernandes dos Santos thesis](https://lume.ufrgs.br/bitstream/handle/10183/234971/001136966.pdf)). The same rho could therefore serve L1 or the RF within about ±20%. What differs is residency: register and L1 contents live for microseconds, so their per-trial exposure is far smaller even at equal per-bit rates.

## Heavy ions and protons change the ratio, not just the rate

If the campaign targets orbit, as the REMU-derived ladder suggests, the terrestrial rho does not transfer. At high LET both technologies saturate near their sensitive area. A Snapdragon 835 L2 measured **about 1×10⁻¹¹ cm²/bit** flat from LET 2 to 6.9 MeV·cm²/mg ([Guertin, NEPP ETW 2020](https://nepp.nasa.gov/docs/etw/2020/17-JUN-WED/1130-Guertin-NEPP-ETW-CL20-2459-ARM-v5.pdf)). Five COTS DDR4 parts, including two Micron dies, saturate at **4.4×10⁻¹²–1.14×10⁻¹⁰ cm²/bit** ([Dufour et al. RADECS 2022](https://www.altertechnology.fr/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Compendium-of-SEE-and-TID-Test-Results-for-DDR4-SDRAM-memories_Paper.pdf)). That puts the heavy-ion rho at **≈0.1–2**: under galactic-cosmic-ray ions, DRAM is not intrinsically more robust per bit.

Protons behave more like neutrons for SRAM. On 16 nm UltraScale+, 64 MeV protons gave BRAM 1.1×10⁻¹⁵ cm²/bit, the same order as its neutron values ([Agiakatsikas et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.08098); [Alexandrescu et al., ESA](https://indico.esa.int/event/300/contributions/6085/attachments/4167/6213/Applicability_of_FinFET_Technologies_for_Space_Applications.pdf)). No per-bit proton DRAM number for LPDDR, GDDR or DDR4 was retrievable, so a proton rho cannot be computed. The qualitative evidence points well above 1. On Jetson Xavier under 200 MeV protons, "the largest contribution to the overall SEE cross-section was the L2 and L3 SEU events", and no GPU errors were logged ([Rodriguez-Ferrandez et al. IOLTS'22](https://upcommons.upc.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/80d67a8c-34a3-408b-aaf1-5bc5bc48b1c1/content)). On Orin NX at 480 MeV, irradiating the LPDDR5 packages produced interface errors and kernel panics, but "no discernible memory data corruption" ([Rodriguez-Ferrandez et al. IOLTS'24](https://upcommons.upc.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/ef814395-1619-43ee-ba00-6a89f0c16d83/content)). One cross-check also shows how an assumed DRAM rate can sit above SRAM. The BSC/ESA proton-only LEO estimate for Xavier L2/L3 is **2–9×10⁻⁹ /bit/day**, which is 10–50× below REMU's 10⁻⁷ /bit/day ([Rodriguez-Ferrandez 2022, Table V](https://upcommons.upc.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/80d67a8c-34a3-408b-aaf1-5bc5bc48b1c1/content)). Against a REMU-anchored DRAM ladder, an "honest" rho could even fall below 1. That estimate excludes heavy ions, though, so it is a lower bound.

Thermal neutrons move the terrestrial number by up to about 2×. The 16 nm BRAM shows 4.7 FIT/Mb thermal against 20 FIT/Mb total ([Xilinx UG116](https://0x04.net/~mwk/xidocs/ug/drr.pdf)). DDR3/DDR4 are thermal-sensitive, and thermal neutrons produced 29% of a K20's SDC FIT at Leadville ([Oliveira et al. ETS 2020](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/1756791)) and up to 59% for some device/code pairs ([Oliveira et al. TNS 2020](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/8975983/)). No per-structure GPU thermal cross sections exist, so the sign of the shift in rho is unknown.

## The equal-window assumption hides a time-scale factor

The formula BER_cache = rho × BER_DRAM carries an implicit assumption that the campaign should make explicit. Write the physical rates as λ_S (SRAM) and λ_D (DRAM) per bit per unit time, with rho = λ_S/λ_D. The DRAM ladder value is BER_DRAM = λ_D × T_acc, where T_acc is the time over which the resident DRAM copy accumulates flips: since load, last scrub, or the start of the beam exposure. All those faults are present for the whole trial. The cache model counts n_cache = BER_cache × R_eff_bits, where R_eff is the time-averaged number of model bits resident in L2. This is the residency-weighted exposure of ACE lifetime analysis, in which AVF is "the fraction of the bit's lifetime during which the bit contained ACE state" ([Biswas et al. ISCA'05](https://liberty.princeton.edu/Publications/isca32_avf.pdf)). By Little's law it is exact: the expected number of flips that land on resident model bits during a window T_win is λ_S × R_eff × T_win. Each flip then vanishes at eviction, because weight lines are clean and are refetched from the DRAM copy. The faithful expression is therefore

**BER_cache = λ_S × T_win = rho × BER_DRAM × (T_win / T_acc).**

The two clocks coincide only in a **beam-equivalent trial**, where one run is exposed to one fluence and DRAM and L2 both collect upsets during that run. Then T_win = T_acc and the simple form holds. That is the right reading for an accelerated stress experiment. Even there, the campaign slightly over-weights DRAM, because it injects all DRAM faults at t = 0 rather than spreading their arrival across the run.

In a **deployment** reading, the ladder represents flips that piled up in DRAM-resident weights over hours to months, while an L2 line holds a given flip for only one inference or one residency period. The ladder's own scale shows which reading it implies. At REMU's 10⁻⁷ /bit/day, BER_DRAM = 10⁻⁸ corresponds to about 2.4 hours of accumulation and 10⁻⁵ to about 100 days. At the terrestrial assumption of 12.51 FIT/Gb (1.25×10⁻¹⁷ per bit-hour), 10⁻⁸ would take about 90,000 years. The ladder is therefore either a space-accumulation model or a heavily accelerated one. In the deployment reading, BER_cache stops depending on the DRAM ladder at all. Take λ_D = 10⁻⁷ /bit/day (1.16×10⁻¹² /s), rho = 1,000 and a 50 ms inference: BER_cache ≈ 6×10⁻¹¹. Even if the whole 72 MiB L2 (6.0×10⁸ bits) held model data, that is **about 0.03 cache flips per inference**. Meanwhile, a 1 GB model at BER_DRAM = 10⁻⁶ carries about 8,600 persistent DRAM flips. Cache faults are then physically negligible unless lines stay resident across very many inferences. Even in that case the window is the residency time, which is still far shorter than T_acc.

The campaign should state which reading it uses. Under the beam-equivalent reading, rho × BER_DRAM is correct and the sweep measures how much an L2 under the same fluence adds. Under the deployment reading, that sweep is a deliberate **upper-bound stress test**, and the paper should say so. Two implementation details follow. First, `round(BER_cache × R_eff_bits)` truncates to zero whenever the expectation is below 0.5. That happens at low rungs and small R_eff, for example R_eff = 10⁷ bits at BER_cache = 10⁻⁸, and silently removes cache faults from the bottom of the ladder. Drawing **n_cache ~ Poisson(BER_cache × R_eff)** preserves the expectation and gives correct trial-to-trial variance. Second, the expected injection count should be logged next to the drawn count, so readers can compare rungs.

## A four-point rho sweep is more defensible than any single value

A single rho cannot be defended. The supportable values span about five decades: ~0.1–2 for heavy ions, ~40–260 from healthy-card field data, and ~10²–10⁴ (central ~10³) from terrestrial component data. None of them was measured on a GPU L2 at 4N or on GDDR6X. Picking one value would hide the campaign's largest uncertainty inside a constant. A log sweep turns that uncertainty into a reported result. The recommended core set is **rho ∈ {1, 10, 100, 1000}**, with each value labelled by what it represents. **1** is the memory-agnostic, REMU-consistent, heavy-ion-like floor. **10** has no direct anchor. It fills the gap between decades, so the response curve is sampled where mixed spectra (heavy ions plus protons) would most likely fall. **100** is the Titan field-consistent value. **1000** is the terrestrial-neutron FinFET central estimate. An optional **10⁴** point covers the upper tail of the node-scaling range, a fast-process-corner 5 nm SRAM against the best DRAM. If the paper must quote one headline value, use **rho = 1,000 for terrestrial operation** (with 100–10,000 as the stated range) and **rho ≈ 1 for space heavy-ion-dominated environments**. Values below 1 add little, because at rho = 1 the cache already receives only the R_eff/N_model fraction of the DRAM flip count.

The resulting BER_cache grid is below. Each cell gives BER_cache and, in parentheses, the expected n_cache at an **illustrative** R_eff of 10⁸ bits (about 12 MiB time-averaged). The measured per-process R_eff should replace this number.

| BER_DRAM \ rho | 1 | 10 | 100 | 1000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1e-8 | 1e-8 (1) | 1e-7 (10) | 1e-6 (100) | 1e-5 (1,000) |
| 1e-7 | 1e-7 (10) | 1e-6 (100) | 1e-5 (1,000) | 1e-4 (10⁴) |
| 1e-6 | 1e-6 (100) | 1e-5 (1,000) | 1e-4 (10⁴) | 1e-3 (10⁵) |
| 1e-5 | 1e-5 (1,000) | 1e-4 (10⁴) | 1e-3 (10⁵) | 1e-2 (10⁶) |

The bottom-right corner flips 1% of resident L2 bits. That is beyond the independent single-upset regime and serves only as a saturation check. The most useful output of the sweep is not any single cell but the **crossover rho***, where cache-induced and DRAM-induced SDC rates are equal: **rho* = (N_DRAM × P_SDC|DRAM) / (R_eff × P_SDC|cache)**. Here N_DRAM is the number of model bits resident in DRAM, and the two P values are per-flip SDC probabilities that the campaign measures itself. Reporting rho* per model lets readers apply their own environment's rho. The report can then say that L2 faults matter for a model only if the true rho exceeds rho*, and that the literature puts rho near 10³ on the ground and near 1 under heavy ions.

If the DRAM ladder is meant to represent physical events, it should carry the larger byte- and word-aligned multi-bit fraction.

## Conclusion

The question "what is the L2-to-GDDR6X per-bit upset ratio?" has no measured answer, and the honest answer depends on the environment rather than being a constant. That ratio rose from about 1 in the SDRAM era to about 10³ for terrestrial neutrons today, while it stays near 1 under heavy ions. The more consequential finding is about time rather than rho. Because the DRAM ladder encodes accumulated, persistent damage and the L2 holds a flip only while its line is resident, scaling BER_cache from BER_DRAM is physically correct only for equal-fluence (beam-equivalent) trials. Under realistic accumulation, cache flips per inference are orders of magnitude below one. Framing the rho sweep as a stress bound and publishing rho* per model makes the campaign's cache results robust to whatever rho turns out to be.

The single experiment that would remove most of this uncertainty is straightforward to describe. Run an L2-resident pattern kernel and a GDDR6X-resident pattern kernel on an RTX 4090 with ECC off, in the same neutron session, with the spot covering die and memory, and filter repeat-address DRAM errors. Until someone does that, any paper that reports a single rho for this platform is reporting an assumption.
